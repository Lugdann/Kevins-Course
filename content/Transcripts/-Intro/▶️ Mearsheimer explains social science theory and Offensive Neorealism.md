(Welcome to A Conversation with History. I'm Harry Kreisler of the Institute of International Studies. Our guest today is John J. Mearsheimer, the R. Wendell Harrison Distinguished Service Professor at the University of Chicago. He's the author of numerous books including Conventional Deterrence, Liddell Hart and the Weight of History, and The Tragedy of Great Power Politics.

  

Where were you born and raised?

I was born in Brooklyn, New York, in December 1947, just as the Cold War was getting started. I was raised in New York City until I was eight years old, at which point my parents moved to the suburbs—Westchester County, Croton, New York.

  

How did your parents shape your character?

My parents were actually not at all interested in politics, so they had almost no influence on pushing me into political science. What they did teach me was to be very honest—a truth-teller—and to work very hard. They believed that God put you on Earth to work hard.

  

Any books that affected you as a young person?

Yes. A handful, but one in particular: David Halberstam’s The Best and the Brightest. I was in the military from 1965 to 1975, the Vietnam War years. I often asked, “How in God’s name did we get into this horrible war?” That book gave a powerful explanation and had a significant impact on my thinking.

  

How did you wind up at West Point?

I joined the Army in 1965. After my first year as an enlisted man, I had two choices: go to West Point or go to Vietnam. They often offered appointments to West Point to enlisted men who showed talent. I initially wanted to go to Vietnam—I didn’t like the military, didn’t like shaving, uniforms, guns, or authority. But my father really wanted me to go to West Point, and, well, he “broke both my arms” to convince me.

  

What was positive about the military?

It taught me a lot about the field I study today. And personally, it taught me to suffer—to work hard, push through adversity, and get up every day to wrestle with the bear. It also taught me to tell the truth, even when it goes against the grain. That’s vital for an academic.

  

How did being in the military affect you during the Vietnam War?

It was tough. I was at West Point from 1966 to 1970. When I went home, I’d face hostility from old friends who’d grown radicalized. I marched in Armed Forces Day parades in NYC, often while demonstrators threw bags of pig’s blood or urine at us and spat or screamed. It was searing.

  

What did you learn about the military as political power?

First, that militaries are conservative—they don’t like war. Officers know they're the ones who’ll die. Second, there's corruption, like in any large institution. The Vietnam War had a corrupting effect on the military.

  

What does it take to be an international relations theorist?

Theorists are born, not made. You need creativity, courage to make controversial arguments, and a deep understanding of history. The first two are innate; the third can be learned.

  

What is a realist theory?

Realists believe the state is the principal actor and that states are obsessed with the balance of power—mostly military power. There are three kinds: human nature realists (like Morgenthau), defensive realists (like Waltz), and offensive realists like me. I believe states seek hegemony; they want to dominate their region. The U.S. is a regional hegemon and doesn’t want peer competitors elsewhere.

  

Why is the U.S. a hegemon?

You can’t be a global hegemon—the world’s too big. But you can dominate your region. The U.S. makes sure no one else dominates theirs. We’ve opposed Nazi Germany, Imperial Japan, the Soviet Union—any peer competitor.

  

What about the post-Cold War optimism?

There were arguments that the state was disappearing and cooperation was replacing conflict. I disagree. Nationalism is alive and well. States remain central. The U.S. kept the peace in Europe and Asia after the Cold War with troop presence. And we’ve fought three wars since: Iraq (1991), Yugoslavia (1999), and Afghanistan (2001+).

  

Why is realism unpopular in America?

Because realism is pessimistic and doesn’t believe in moral exceptionalism. Americans are liberal idealists who believe problems can be solved. Realists say conflict is inevitable and states are all alike—good guys and bad guys is a false distinction.

  

Do we act on realism but speak idealism?

Exactly. We act based on realpolitik, but justify it in liberal terms. Elites speak two languages—one for the public, one behind closed doors. When we allied with Stalin in WWII, we rebranded him “Uncle Joe” and called the USSR an “emerging democracy.”

  

Should citizens be skeptical?

Absolutely. Watch what states do, not what they say. There’s often a huge gap between rhetoric and action.

  

What’s the responsibility of strategists and scholars?

To be honest, free-speaking, and challenge conventional wisdom. Most political scientists have little social responsibility. But we must connect our ideas to public policy debates and help the public understand.

  

What about terrorism?

Realism doesn’t have much to say about non-state actors like al-Qaeda. But terrorism still unfolds within the international system, so realist logic matters. The Bush administration overemphasized military force. We need to win hearts and minds. Diplomacy is more effective than bombs in the long run.

  

What’s your take on China’s rise?

If China keeps growing economically, it will turn that into military power and seek to dominate Asia. The U.S. will try to stop that—just like we did with past peer competitors. A containment coalition is already forming: Japan, Vietnam, India, Russia, and the U.S.

  

Will that lead to conflict?

Potentially. Taiwan is a big flashpoint. So are border disputes with Russia and control over sea lanes. A powerful China will crash into a powerful U.S.

  

Is there any place for values in realism?

Not really. States pursue power. Samantha Power’s book shows how the U.S. consistently failed to act in humanitarian crises, despite all our rhetoric. You want safety? Get your own state and make it strong.

  

Advice for students?

Read widely. Learn multiple theories—realist, liberal, constructivist. Compare them to history. Don’t just become a realist; figure out what you believe. Stay open-minded. Your theories at 20 will probably change by 40.

  

Final word on education?

Take history. Learn theory. Stay informed. Constantly update your worldview. And never forget: we all have theories in our head—make sure yours match reality.)
