(So what is the new realist theory? Neorealism is a theory that basically says that states behave according to the structure of the international system. It's how states are organized and what the architecture of the international system looks like that largely influences how those states behave. The key organizing principle of the international system is anarchy. Anarchy basically says that there is no higher authority above states. States are like balls on a pool table - they have no higher authority that sits above them. The neorealist or structural realist argument is that in the absence of a higher authority that can protect states if they get into trouble, those states will compete with each other for power because power really matters for your survival. If you're a state in the international system, you want to be able to survive. Survival has to be your highest goal because if you don't survive, you can't pursue any of your other goals. But survival can be a very tricky goal in a system where there's no higher authority that can protect you if trouble comes. To deal with that problem, you try to make yourself as powerful as possible. You want to look like Godzilla because if you're Godzilla in the international system, you're really powerful. It's highly unlikely that any other state will attack you. This is the basic insight of neorealism - that the structure or architecture of the international system (focusing mainly on anarchy here) leaves states with little choice but to compete among themselves for power, because the more power you have, the more likely you are to survive. Why do we need theories? Why do we need neorealist theory and which phenomena do we want to explain with it? We need theories to understand how the world works. There's no question that the world is an amazingly complicated place, and this includes international politics. For any individual, whether a policymaker or a student of international politics, you're trying to make sense of the world. You're trying to figure out what are the principal forces driving state behavior - how can I get State A to do this, how can I get State B to do that. To do that you need theories. Theories are simplifications of reality. Given this complicated world, what you want are theories which simplify that reality and allow you to make sense of it. What's nice about neorealism is that it is a very simple theory that people can use to help figure out how the world operates, and that allows people not only to understand the world but to think about what are the right or wrong policies that states should pursue for their own benefit. In which field of political science is neorealism mostly used and can create a great advantage? Neorealism is mainly used in the subfield of international relations, and within that subfield it tends to be employed more by people who study international security than international political economy. I do believe realism has a lot to say about political economy, but the fact is that it's people who study questions of war and peace, people who study international security, that mainly focus on realism. That's the domain where realism has its greatest impact - in the international security realm. What are the main limitations and critiques toward neorealism? The main limitation is that like any theory, it can't explain everything. My view is that theories (even the best theories, and I consider neorealism among them) can only explain about 75 percent of what happens in the world. That means neorealism is wrong about 25 percent of the time. Why is this the case? It's actually quite simple - we're dealing with a really complicated world that's very hard to make sense of. We invent theories which are simplifications of reality that help us understand that complicated world. If you're going to simplify reality and come up with a simple theory (and simple theories are the best theories), that means you're going to leave some factors on the cutting room floor. Some factors that matter in international politics are not incorporated into the theory because they're deemed not very important. Neorealism pays little attention to domestic politics - it says you don't need domestic politics to understand how the world works. It's the structure of the system that's largely determinative. Sometimes domestic politics really matters, and that factor you left out jumps up and influences events. In those cases, neorealism fails to explain what's going on because it can't account for domestic politics. Domestic politics is left out of neorealism, but that's not to say it never matters. Neorealists argue it only matters sometimes, and not very often, so they can afford to leave it out. But this doesn't deny there will be occasions where domestic politics matters. So neorealism, like all theories, is a simplification of reality, and when simplifying a complicated reality, there will be instances where neorealism fails the test - as would be true of all theories. Why is it important that students study neorealism? The main reason is that it's among the most powerful theories we have in international relations. I would argue (not surprisingly) that it is the most powerful theory. If you want to understand how international politics works, you can do no better than to focus on neorealism. For example, if you're interested in whether China can rise peacefully, you need a theory to answer that question (since we have no facts about the future). I would maintain neorealism is the best theory for capturing this question. It's a powerful theory students should understand. Now, this isn't to say every listener should conclude neorealism is the best theory - some might prefer liberal theories like democratic peace theory. But you should understand neorealism because it's one of the most important theories, and many important scholars consider it among the best IR theories. What are some key readings on neorealism?
